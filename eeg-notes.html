What EEG measures

EEG is a recording of electrical potential differences. Scalp electrodes detect the combined effects of neural currents and other electrical sources, rather than the activity of a single neuron. The voltage at an electrode is measured relative to a reference; changing the reference can change the appearance of a waveform.

EEG’s main advantage for this research is timing. It can follow changes at millisecond resolution, allowing questions about early attention and later processing. It offers less direct information about exactly where those changes originate. Conductive tissues spread electrical fields, and different source configurations can produce similar scalp patterns.

EEG is not a recording of a person’s thoughts. A larger voltage does not simply mean “more brain activity”, and scalp polarity does not identify neural excitation or inhibition.

Background: Buzsáki and colleagues, on the origins of electrical signals.

From continuous EEG to an ERP

A continuous EEG trace contains ongoing activity throughout a recording. To estimate an ERP, researchers align short segments, or epochs, to repeated events. For example, zero milliseconds may mark the measured onset of a picture. Averaging within a condition emphasises activity that is consistently time-locked to that event.

The average is a summary. It can hide meaningful differences between trials, and a small average may reflect variable timing rather than an absent response. Trial counts and noise levels affect the precision of estimates.

A component is usually described through its polarity, timing, scalp distribution and sensitivity to an experimental contrast. Its name is a convention, not a guarantee that one isolated psychological process has been measured. Positive and negative plotting directions also vary across figures, so always read the axis labels.

Mean amplitude over a defined time window often answers a different question from peak amplitude or latency. Decide which quantity matches the hypothesis, and avoid selecting a window just because the current data show the largest difference there.

Neural timing and emotional recovery

Reactivity, maintenance and recovery require different observations. A comparison immediately after stimulus onset cannot establish how quickly someone returns to baseline. Likewise, a large late response during an ongoing emotional image does not establish that it will persist once that image disappears.

Record stimulus onset and offset, responses and relevant task transitions. Leave enough time after the emotional event to measure the proposed recovery process, and consider carry-over into the next trial. A pre-stimulus baseline may already contain residual activity from the preceding event.

Recovery needs an operational definition: for example, a decline across prespecified post-offset intervals relative to a suitable comparison condition. Failure to find a significant difference in a late interval is not proof that two conditions have become equivalent.

As an empirical starting point, Hajcak and Olvet’s study of attention during and after emotional pictures illustrates why activity after picture offset is worth examining. It is background literature, not a finding from this website’s research programme.

Experimental paradigms

Examples of task families and the questions they can support
Task familyQuestionDesign consideration
Emotional picture viewingHow does processing differ between emotional and comparison stimuli?Control visual properties, arousal, instructions and presentation duration; include a post-offset period for recovery.
Cueing and visual searchWhat attracts attention, and how readily does it shift?Separate capture from disengagement and response preparation. Gaze and response time need not reflect the same process.
Oddball tasksHow are unusual or task-relevant events processed?Rarity, physical differences and target status can each influence the response.
Flanker or go/no-go tasksHow do conflict, errors and response demands affect processing?Balance performance demands and obtain enough relevant trials; rare errors can yield imprecise estimates.
Feedback and reward tasksHow are outcomes evaluated?Consider expectation, outcome probability, reward magnitude and the comparison used.

These examples describe methods in the wider field; they are not a list of active AffectNeuroLab studies. ERP CORE offers externally developed tasks for learning how experimental comparisons isolate responses, including visual search, oddball and flanker paradigms.

A considered preprocessing workflow

Preprocessing prepares a recording for a particular analysis. There is no single set of settings that is optimal for every component, population or task. Keep raw data unchanged and record the order and parameters of each operation.

  1. Check the recording and events. Inspect channel labels, sampling rate, reference information, missing data and event codes. Compare recorded events with the task log; check hardware delays and timing jitter.
  2. Inspect data quality. Review time series, spectra, noisy channels and movement. Record why channels or segments are unsuitable. A quiet-looking trace is not sufficient evidence of a valid recording.
  3. Choose filters deliberately. High-pass filters attenuate slow variation; low-pass filters attenuate faster variation. Filter settings can distort amplitude, timing and sustained responses. Check the frequency response and boundary handling, and preserve the frequencies needed for the question.
  4. Address artifacts. Blinks, eye movements, muscle activity and electrical noise require inspection. Independent component analysis can help separate some sources, but component classification is an informed decision. Review spatial patterns, spectra and time courses rather than treating an automatic label as definitive.
  5. Manage channels and reference. Document rejected or interpolated channels and the reference used for analysis. Interpolation estimates missing signals; it does not restore the original measurement.
  6. Epoch and choose a baseline. Align data to the relevant event and include intervals long enough for the hypothesis. Baseline subtraction changes all values in an epoch; a baseline affected by anticipation or a preceding stimulus can alter the interpretation.
  7. Apply quality criteria consistently. Document rejected trials, exclusions and retained trial counts by condition. Unequal loss of data can change precision and potentially bias comparisons.
  8. Inspect the final result. Review individual and group waveforms, scalp distributions and condition differences. Check whether results depend strongly on plausible processing decisions.

Technical guidance: EEGLAB filtering, ICA and artifact inspection, and MNE tutorials. These notes explain decisions rather than prescribing universal numerical settings.

Affective processing and candidate markers

An EEG or ERP measure becomes useful through a well-defined contrast, reliable measurement and a justified interpretation. The following examples are often discussed in affective and cognitive neuroscience. None uniquely identifies depression, anxiety or an emotional state.

Late positive potential (LPP)
A sustained positive-going response often larger for emotionally engaging than neutral images. It is useful for questions about continued processing, but is influenced by task goals and stimulus properties. Sustained activity should not automatically be equated with unsuccessful regulation or slow recovery.
P3 / P300
A family of positive responses associated with aspects of task relevance, attention and evaluation. The paradigm and contrast determine the interpretation; a smaller P3 does not by itself identify a deficit or disorder.
Error-related negativity (ERN)
A response-locked negative deflection associated with errors in suitable tasks. Research uses it to investigate performance monitoring. Error count, accuracy, task demands and reliability matter when comparing individuals.
Reward-related responses
Feedback-locked contrasts can help investigate outcome evaluation. Results depend on how rewarding and non-rewarding outcomes are defined and compared, as well as participants’ expectations.
Oscillatory activity
Analyses of frequency-dependent power or phase address aspects of the signal that an ERP average does not capture. Band definitions, baseline choices, artifacts and task context must be specified. Frequency bands should not be treated as labels for single mental states.

Further reading: Hajcak and Foti on the LPP and P300; ERP CORE examples of P3b and ERN measurement. These external references are not lab publications.

Strengths, limitations and inference

EEG offers high temporal resolution and can be combined with behavioural tasks and other measurements. Repeated trials allow a close examination of how a response changes over a task. These strengths are particularly relevant when an explanation concerns timing rather than only average performance.

Interpretation is constrained by artifacts, source ambiguity, measurement error and overlap between processes. A group difference does not establish a cause, and an effect observed across groups may be too unreliable to predict an individual’s symptoms. Associations with symptom scores also need to account for the design, sample and possible confounding influences.

Statistical significance does not provide an effect’s practical importance. Report effect sizes and uncertainty, address the number of comparisons and distinguish planned tests from exploratory analyses. A replication or independent validation asks more of a proposed marker than a difference in a single sample.

For research on recovery, combine the neural observation with a clear account of what has ended, what remains on screen, what the participant is doing and what “baseline” means. Behaviour, gaze and reported experience can constrain the explanation.

What to report and retain

  • Task instructions, stimuli, event definitions and timing verification.
  • Recording configuration, reference, sampling rate and electrode information.
  • Software versions, filter specifications and every processing step.
  • Channel, component, trial and participant exclusions with reasons.
  • Retained trial counts, analysis windows, electrodes and outcome definitions.
  • Statistical model, comparisons, uncertainty and the status of exploratory analyses.
  • Data and code availability, with consent and privacy constraints respected.

EEG-BIDS provides a standard structure for recording metadata, events and associated files. Clear organisation supports later checking and reuse; it does not replace quality control.
